Gutenberg is often remembered as the inventor of a press. The mechanical act of pressing paper, however, was only one part of the system.

The larger economic change came from breaking a page into reusable characters. Once a page had been printed, the type could be dismantled, sorted and composed again for another page.

That changed the cost structure of making text.

## Manuscript copying scales almost directly with the number of copies

If a scribe needs (T) hours to reproduce a text, a simple approximation for (n) copies is

$$
C_{copy}\approx nT
$$

Each additional copy requires another large block of labour.

Woodblock printing already changed this relationship because one carved block could make many impressions. But a full page block was largely tied to that page. New content required new carving.

Movable type made the reusable unit much smaller: the individual character.

## A page became an assembly of reusable parts

A typeset page can be thought of, loosely, as

$$
\text{page}=\sum \text{reusable characters}+\text{spacing and layout rules}
$$

This is a production model rather than a literal mathematical equation.

Individual pieces of type are composed into lines and pages. After printing, they can return to the type case and be used again.

The cost structure then looks more like

$$
C\approx C_{setup}+nC_{impression}
$$

where setup includes type preparation, composition, proofreading and press preparation, while each additional impression mainly adds paper, ink, press time and handling.

As the print run grows, the setup cost is spread across more copies.

## Why movable type had to be a system

Reusable letters alone are not enough to print a long book reliably.

The process also requires consistent type dimensions, a way to cast large numbers of matching characters, ink that adheres properly to metal, a mechanism that applies reasonably even pressure, and skilled composition and proofreading.

Library of Congress material notes that Gutenberg's metalworking skills helped him cut and cast many types, and that tens of thousands of individual letters were needed when multiple pages were being prepared.

The achievement was therefore not one isolated component. It was the integration of several technologies into repeatable production.

## The press turned composition into replication

Early European presses used screw mechanisms derived from older pressure devices. Once the forme was inked, paper was placed over it and a platen applied pressure.

The press did not determine the text. Its job was to reproduce an already composed surface consistently.

Too little or uneven pressure produced incomplete impressions. Poor registration affected margins and page alignment. Mechanical control turned modular composition into repeatable manufacturing.

## The economic result was a lower marginal cost of copying

The important change was not simply that the first copy could be made faster. It was that, after setup, producing more copies became much cheaper than rewriting an entire book.

Printers therefore faced a different optimisation problem: how large a print run would spread composition and setup costs without creating unsold inventory?

Text production had begun to resemble batch manufacturing, with fixed costs, unit costs and demand risk.

## Why that changed circulation of knowledge

The Library of Congress describes the Gutenberg Bible as the first major Western publication printed with movable metal type and notes the rapid spread of presses in the decades that followed.

When many near-identical copies of a text can circulate, readers in different places can refer to the same edition more easily. Errors can also be multiplied, but stable replication itself changes scholarly, religious, administrative and commercial communication.

Classical texts, religious works, legal material, teaching texts, scientific arguments and political pamphlets all became easier to reproduce at scale.

## The limits of the transformation

Printing did not instantly make books cheap or literacy universal. Paper, type, presses, labour and distribution still required capital. Literacy, language, censorship and transport shaped who could actually read what was printed.

The deeper change was the production mechanism: high setup effort followed by relatively cheaper repetition.

That pattern later reappeared in very different media. Recorded music, film, software and digital content all separate the cost of producing an original from the cost of making another copy.

In that sense, Gutenberg's system was not merely a machine. It was an early industrial model for information products.
